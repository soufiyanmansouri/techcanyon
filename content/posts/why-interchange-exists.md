Swipe or tap a debit card for coffee and a small percentage of the sale leaves the merchant’s side of the table. That slice is often called interchange: revenue for the card issuer, mostly invisible to the shopper. Understanding it is how you read a payments business without drowning in acronyms.

## Who pays whom

A typical card purchase involves the merchant, the merchant’s acquirer/processor, the card network, and the issuer (the bank or fintech that gave you the card). Interchange is a fee structure that generally flows toward the issuer as compensation for funding, fraud risk, and the payment guarantee merchants rely on. Networks also take a piece. Merchants feel the total merchant discount rate; customers usually feel nothing at the register.

The invisibility is the point. If shoppers saw a line item every time, behavior would change. So the economics live in B2B contracts and scheme rules.

## Why fintechs care

Fintechs that issue branded cards often share in interchange. More spend can mean more revenue — which is why cash-back and rewards can feel “free” until you follow the money. Rewards are frequently funded, in part, by that merchant-side economics. That does not make rewards a scam. It does mean the business model is interdependent: merchant costs, consumer perks, and issuer margins are linked.

Regulation can cap or reshape interchange in some markets and card types. When the fee pool shrinks, issuers look to subscriptions, credit interest, or thinner rewards. Product strategy tracks policy as closely as it tracks design.

## Debit vs credit, online vs offline

Not all taps are equal. Card type, channel, authentication method, and merchant category can change the rate. E-commerce and card-not-present flows carry different risk profiles than a tap at a café. That is why “interchange” is a family of schedules, not a single number you memorize once.

For founders building checkout, the practical question is total cost of acceptance and authorization performance — not winning a semantic debate about fee names.

## How to read a pitch deck

If a company says it “makes money on cards,” ask: issuing or acquiring? Consumer or commercial? Who takes fraud losses? How sensitive is the model to interchange regulation? Vague answers are a smell. Clear answers mean they understand the rails they sit on.

## Bottom line

Interchange exists because card payments shift risk and convenience onto a network of parties that need to get paid. Shoppers get speed. Merchants get guaranteed funds (with caveats). Issuers get a fee stream. Fintechs that ride that stream must stay honest about where the money comes from — especially when they market rewards as magic.

## Deep dive: reliability is a feature merchants will pay for

A payment platform’s brand is the authorization rate at 6 p.m. on a peak Saturday. Dashboards that explain declines, retries that respect issuer rules, and reconciliation files that match finance’s expectations reduce hidden labor. When you evaluate processors, ask for incident history, settlement timing distribution, and how chargeback evidence is packaged — not only the sticker fee.

Builders should also plan for partial outages: degrade gracefully, queue safely, and communicate in human language. Merchants forgive a brief blip with a status page. They do not forgive silence while carts die.
